The freelance tax deductions checklist (2026)

Before anything else: tax rules differ by country and change yearly, and this article is general education, not tax advice. Use it as a conversation checklist for an accountant in your jurisdiction — an hour of their time usually pays for itself several times over.

Most freelancers don't overpay tax because of exotic loopholes they missed — they overpay because ordinary, obviously deductible expenses never got recorded. This checklist covers the categories that most commonly slip through, roughly in order of how much money people leave on them.

The big ones people still miss

  • Home office. If you work from a dedicated space at home, a proportion of rent or mortgage interest, utilities, internet and insurance is typically claimable — by floor-area share or your country's flat-rate method. The keyword is dedicated; the kitchen table is a harder case. Ask your accountant which method wins for you.
  • The phone and internet split. The business share of your mobile plan and home internet, every month, forever. Small number, twelve times a year, most freelancers claim none of it.
  • Software subscriptions. Everything in your stack: design tools, code editors, invoicing apps, cloud storage, email, hosting, domains, the AI tools you now pay for. Audit your card statements — this category has quietly doubled for most people since 2023.
  • Equipment. Laptop, monitor, desk, chair, camera, headphones. Depending on jurisdiction and price, expensed immediately or depreciated over a few years. Keep receipts for anything with a plug.

The forgotten categories

  • Professional development — courses, books, conference tickets and the travel to them, industry memberships.
  • Professional services — your accountant's fee (deductible, pleasingly), lawyer reviews of your contracts, bookkeeping software.
  • Insurance — professional indemnity / liability cover; in some countries parts of health insurance for the self-employed.
  • Payment and bank fees — Stripe/PayPal cuts, currency conversion, business account fees. They're invisible because they're subtracted before money reaches you — they're still costs.
  • Marketing — your website, portfolio hosting, ads, directory listings, even business cards if you still believe in them.
  • Client-related travel and meals — rules and percentages vary widely by country; log the who/why on every receipt.
  • Bad debts — in some jurisdictions, invoices you've genuinely written off after chasing (see our chase sequence first).
  • Retirement contributions — often the single largest tax lever available to the self-employed, and the one with the longest payoff. Ask specifically about this.

The habit that makes all of this real

A deduction without a record is a deduction you don't have. The minimum viable system is two rules:

  • One card. Every business expense goes on one dedicated card/account. Your bank statement becomes a first draft of your expense report.
  • Fifteen minutes monthly. Same day you send retainer invoices: categorize the month's expenses while you still remember what they were. Tax season becomes an export, not an archaeology dig.

Tools can automate most of this — expense-scanning apps that watch your transactions, or accounting software with bank feeds. We list the ones worth using in the tool directory under Taxes and Invoicing & Accounting.

Connect this to your pricing: whatever your expenses total, they belong in your hourly rate — that's the "annual business expenses" field in our free rate calculator. Deductions soften costs; your rate is what actually pays them.

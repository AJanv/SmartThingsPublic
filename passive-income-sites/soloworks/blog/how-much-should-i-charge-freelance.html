How much should I charge as a freelancer? The 2026 formula

Most freelancers set their rate by taking their old salary, dividing by 2,000 hours, and adding a little. That math feels sensible and it is quietly bankrupting them. Here's the formula that actually works, and why the salary comparison is the most expensive mistake in freelancing.

Why salary math fails

An employee earning $90,000 costs their employer far more than $90,000 — there's payroll tax, insurance, equipment, software, office space, paid holidays, sick days, and training. As a freelancer, you are now the employer. All of those costs moved onto your side of the table.

Worse: an employee is paid for 40 hours a week whether they're in meetings, on a slow day, or waiting on someone else. You only get paid for billable hours — and for most freelancers, only 50–70% of working time is billable. The rest goes to proposals, admin, marketing, and finding the next client.

The four numbers you need

  • Target income — what you want to take home for the year, after tax.
  • Business expenses — software, gear, insurance, accounting, co-working. For most solo freelancers this is $5,000–$15,000/year.
  • True billable hours — working weeks (subtract holidays and sick days: most people land at 44–48) × hours per week × your billable percentage (be honest: 60% is typical).
  • Tax rate — your effective self-employment rate. 25–35% is common; ask an accountant.

The formula

(Target income ÷ (1 − tax rate) + expenses) ÷ billable hours × 1.15

The 1.15 at the end is a 15% buffer for slow months, late payers, and the client who disappears mid-project. It's not padding — it's insurance you will use.

Worked example: $90,000 take-home, 25% tax, $8,000 expenses, 46 weeks × 40 hours × 60% billable = 1,104 billable hours. That's ($120,000 + $8,000) ÷ 1,104 × 1.15 ≈ $134/hour. Notice how far that is from the $45/hour the salary math suggests.

Then adjust for the market, not toward it

The formula gives you your floor — the number below which you're losing money without noticing. Market positioning moves you up from there, not down: specialists charge 2–3× generalists, and outcomes ("a landing page that converts") price higher than time ("40 hours of design").

A simple calibration: if you're winning every proposal, you're too cheap. Raise your quoted rate every two or three new clients until roughly one in four says no on price. That's the market telling you you've found it.

Common questions

Should I charge less while I'm building a portfolio? Charge less for a defined period or project count, never as an open-ended identity. "My rate is $120; for my first three projects in this niche I'm charging $80" preserves the anchor.

Hourly or fixed price? Start hourly until you can estimate accurately, then move to fixed prices for defined projects — fixed pricing pays you for speed and expertise instead of punishing them.
